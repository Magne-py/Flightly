How difficulty is set

Each puzzle is scored on five factors and bucketed into 1★ Simple through 5★ Extreme:

1
Hop count — longer shortest routes are harder.
2
Distinct shortest paths — many alternatives = easier.
3

4

Heads up: a 1★ Simple puzzle can still stump you if there's only one shortest path. What is JetSets?

JetSets is a daily flight-routing puzzle. Each round gives you two airports and asks you to chart the journey between them in as few hops as possible — using only real-world commercial flights.

Type airport codes (or names, or cities) into the search bar, build a candidate route, then submit. The grading lights up each leg so you can refine the path on your next try. You have six attempts.

How a round plays

  • The puzzle header shows your start and destination, the shortest possible hop count, and a 1–5★ difficulty rating.
  • Add up to 5 intermediate stops per row. Leave trailing slots empty for shorter routes.
  • Press Enter to add an airport, Shift+Enter (or Submit) to grade the row, Backspace on an empty input to undo the last stop.
  • Each cell turns green/yellow/orange/red — see the grading rubric below — and you replan from there.

How grading works

After you submit a row, every cell is colored independently:

Green
The airport sits on a shortest route and in the correct slot. When you complete the journey, every filled cell in that row lights up green — the win is the win, regardless of how many stops you used.
Yellow
The airport sits on a shortest route, but either you placed it in a slot the route doesn't use, or the previous airport doesn't have a direct flight to it (so the leg into yellow won't fly). The airport itself is right — what's around it isn't.
Orange
A valid flight exists from the previous airport and you can still reach the destination — just via a longer-than-shortest path.
Red
No flight exists from the previous airport, or no onward path reaches the destination from here.
Purple
The absolute best possible route — shortest hops and the fewest kilometres flown across all shortest-hop options. Only one purple win exists per puzzle, and it pays a 2.0× scoring bonus.
Confusing grading patterns — worked examples

Cells are graded per-slot, independently. Each slot is checked against the single shortest route that best matches your row, so neighbouring cells can disagree in ways that look strange at first. Here are the patterns that surprise most players:

CDG MAD DXB

Green → Orange → Green. Slot 1 (CDG) and slot 3 (DXB) both sit on the shortest path; slot 2 (MAD) doesn't, but a real flight exists from MAD that eventually still reaches the destination. Because each slot is graded independently of its neighbours, a bad middle stop doesn't poison the rest of the row. Fix: swap MAD for whichever airport the shortest route uses between CDG and DXB.

JFK LHR SIN SYD

An all-green row that's longer than the shortest path. Once you complete the journey, every filled cell is repainted green — the win is a win. The result panel will tell you the shortest possible was fewer stops, so you can chase a higher score next round, but the row itself reads as "you got there." Fix: compare against the shortest route shown in the result panel and try to drop a stop next time.

DXB SIN

An all-purple row. You hit the puzzle's shortest hop count and picked the route with the fewest kilometres flown — the single tightest path through the network. Multiple shortest-hop routes can exist, but only one (or a handful tied for fewest km) trigger the purple win and the 2.0× scoring bonus.

XYZ AMS DOH

Yellow after a red leg. Yellow doesn't require the previous leg to fly — if an airport is on a shortest route (just in the wrong slot), we still tell you. Even though the chain leading up to AMS broke, AMS is a useful airport to keep — just reposition it. Fix: move AMS to the slot where it belongs and replace XYZ.

LHR DXB SIN

Green then Yellow — same slot the shortest route uses, but no flight from your previous airport. Yellow doesn't always mean "wrong slot." Here DXB is on a shortest route and sits in the slot where that route puts it — but there's no direct flight from LHR to DXB on this particular shortest path, so the leg between green and yellow is broken. The grader marks DXB yellow rather than green to flag that the connection won't fly, even though the airport itself is right. Fix: either swap LHR for an airport that does fly to DXB, or leave LHR and replace DXB with a hub that LHR connects to and that still leads to the destination.

FRA IST

Two yellows in a row. Both airports are on a shortest route, but neither is in the right slot. Often this means you got the right pair but in reverse, or you need to drop a stop and shift the rest down. Fix: try swapping the two airports' positions next round.

LHR QQQ

Green then red. Slot 1 nailed the shortest route's first hop, but slot 2 is unreachable from LHR — or it can't reach the destination at all. Fix: keep LHR, replace QQQ with a connecting hub from the Learn page connections list.

The cardinal rule: treat each cell as its own verdict. A red doesn't mean everything after it is wrong, and a green doesn't validate the cells before it.

Difficulty stars

Each puzzle is rated 1★ (Simple) to 5★ (Extreme). The score combines:

  • Hop count — longer shortest routes are harder.
  • Number of distinct shortest paths — many alternatives = easier.
  • Endpoint fame — well-known hubs (Paris, JFK, etc.) are easier to anchor on.
  • Intermediate fame — having a famous airport on the shortest path makes it findable.
  • Slot tightness — narrow slot choices are harder.

Final ratings are bucketed into quintiles, so the distribution is balanced across the 1–5★ range.

How scoring works

Solving a puzzle pays out points based on three multiplicative factors:

  • Difficulty base — 1★ pays 50, 2★ pays 80, 3★ pays 120, 4★ pays 180, 5★ pays 260. Harder puzzles are worth more headline points.
  • Attempt curve — full credit on try 1, then a smooth linear taper down to 1/6 credit on try 6. Solve faster, score more.
  • Optimal-route bonus — match the shortest hop count exactly and the payout is multiplied by 1.5×. A win that takes extra stops still counts, just without the bonus.
  • Best-route bonus (purple) — match the shortest hop count and fly the fewest possible kilometres (the single tightest route across all shortest-hop options) and the payout is multiplied by 2.0×. The cells light up purple, and only this exact route earns the tier.

Worked examples: a 5★ Extreme nailed in 1 try with the optimal route earns 390 pts. The same puzzle solved in 1 try on the best (purple) route earns 520 pts. A 3★ Medium solved on try 2 with the optimal route earns 150 pts. A 1★ Simple solved on try 6 without the optimal route earns 8 pts. Speedrun stacks every solve into a running total across the 90-second window.

Hints & strategy

  • Anchor on hubs. Major airports (Heathrow, Frankfurt, Doha, Atlanta, Singapore…) connect to almost everything — guessing one early often gives you a quick yellow/green.
  • Mind the geography. Direct flights tend to follow great-circle paths. If your start and destination are on opposite sides of an ocean, ask which hub the airlines actually fly through.
  • Use the colors. A row that's all orange tells you the path is valid but not shortest — try replacing one airport with a more-connected hub. Yellow tells you the airport itself is right but either in the wrong slot or not reachable from the previous airport — try repositioning it, or swap the airport before it.
  • The Learn page is your friend. Open it from the menu to explore which destinations any airport flies to.
  • Try modes. "By Layovers" lets you train on routes of a specific length. "By Continent" restricts the graph to a region — useful for learning a part of the world. "Cryptic" hides the map and city names for pure code-recall practice.

A note from the dev

Hey, and thanks for playing. A few honest things worth sharing before you go any deeper.

The flight network powering JetSets comes from the OpenFlights project, which hasn't been actively maintained for several years now. That means some routes are out of date — you'll occasionally hit a "no flight exists" wall on a connection real-world airlines fly today, or find a long-since-discontinued route still in the graph. I'm working on a refresh that pulls 2025 schedules from a more current source; in the meantime, treat the puzzle as a thoughtful approximation of the world's air network rather than gospel.

I built this on weekends because I wanted a geography game that rewarded learning the shape of global aviation — which hubs feed which regions, where the long-haul corridors run, which countries you can only reach by stitching together two unglamorous flights. The Daily is meant to be a small ritual. The Speedrun is the same idea with the safeties off. The Learn page is a quiet way to wander.

On difficulty: I tried hard to make this fair to a casual player while leaving the door wide open for anyone who wants to get really good. The Daily should be solvable on a coffee break with patience and an educated guess about which hub the airlines actually use. The mode menu opens up the rest — Random for variety, By Layovers when you want to drill a specific muscle, By Continent for regional specialisation, Cryptic when you're ready to drop the map and the city names, Speedrun when you want to see how good you've actually gotten. Purple grading sits at the very top: it's not enough to reach the destination — you have to find the absolute tightest route through the network.

I believe in games that quietly reward mastery. The same puzzle should feel different to you on day one and after six months of play, and the journey between those two versions of you should be entirely yours. JetSets tries to keep the floor low and the ceiling high; the middle is up to you.

If something feels broken, a route looks wrong, or you have an idea for what would make this better — I'd love to hear it. And if you've ever found yourself thinking "ah, that's how you'd fly Buenos Aires to Reykjavik," then JetSets is doing its job.

Happy travels.
— dev
